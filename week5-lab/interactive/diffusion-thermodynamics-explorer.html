Nothing on this tab is learned. The forward process is given, from physics, and it is the piece of the problem nobody had to fit. That is what makes the remaining learning easy.
The physical picture. A drop of ink in water. Molecular collisions push it about at random, sharp structure dissolves, and eventually the glass is uniform. That final state is equilibrium, and it has forgotten where the drop was. Run the slider to 200 and you have destroyed the dataset. The clever part is that each individual step added only a little noise, so each individual step is nearly reversible.

Drop the reverse steps to 5 and generate. The samples are blurred, because each step now has to undo far more than it can. That is the trade at the heart of every diffusion model: quality against the number of network calls, and it is why fast samplers are their own research area.
Why noise is added back on the way out. Setting the last slider to 0 makes every point run downhill to the nearest peak of the density, and the cloud collapses onto a few spots. The injected noise is what keeps the samples spread across the distribution instead of piling up at its modes. It is the same reason a physical system at finite temperature explores its state space rather than freezing into one configuration.

Which way is "towards the data"?

Each arrow is the score: the direction in which the density increases fastest.

The vocabulary this borrows

The reverse of a diffusion needs the gradient of the log density. That is Langevin dynamics, which physicists have used to sample from Boltzmann distributions since long before machine learning existed. The forward process obeys the Fokker-Planck equation. None of this vocabulary was invented here.
Drag the step slider from 199 down to 0 and watch the arrow field sharpen. Early in the reverse process the density is nearly Gaussian and the arrows all point roughly to the middle, so coarse structure is recovered first. Late in the process the arrows resolve fine detail. A diffusion model builds an image the way a photograph develops: the big shapes arrive before the edges.
